\section{Setup}

A periodic cell $\Om$ splits into two subdomains meeting on an interface $\Gam$,
\[
\Om=\Om_1\cup\Om_2,\qquad \Om_1\cap\Om_2=\varnothing,\qquad
\Gam=\partial\Om_1\cap\partial\Om_2,
\]
with constant conductivities $K_1,K_2>0$ and $\bn$ the unit normal on $\Gam$
pointing out of $\Om_1$. Homogenization gives the effective tensor $\Keff$ from
a \emph{corrector} (cell) problem posed on $\Om$. There are two ways to write
that problem, and this note shows they are the same one. Nothing below uses any
property of the phases beyond $K_1,K_2$ being positive constants.

\paragraph{Notation.}
$\chi$ is the \emph{indicator function} of $\Om_1$: the function equal to $1$
inside $\Om_1$ and $0$ outside,
\begin{equation}
\chi(\mathbf x)=\mathbf 1_{\Om_1}(\mathbf x)=
\begin{cases} 1, & \mathbf x\in\Om_1,\\ 0, & \mathbf x\in\Om_2.\end{cases}
\label{eq:chi}
\end{equation}
$\varphi$ is the phase field: a smooth function equal to $1$ in $\Om_1$ and $0$
in $\Om_2$, sweeping between them across a band of decay length $\varepsilon$.
It is a smoothed $\chi$. $\avg{\cdot}=|\Om|^{-1}\!\int_\Om(\cdot)$, and
$\jump{\,\cdot\,}$ is the jump across $\Gam$.

\paragraph{Problem A (sharp).} Find \emph{two} cell-periodic fields, $\bt_1$
defined on $\Om_1$ only and $\bt_2$ defined on $\Om_2$ only, with
\begin{align}
\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(K_1(\nabla\bt_1+\bI)\big)&=\mathbf 0 \ \text{in }\Om_1, &
\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(K_2(\nabla\bt_2+\bI)\big)&=\mathbf 0 \ \text{in }\Om_2,
\label{eq:A-bulk}\\
\bt_1&=\bt_2 \ \text{on }\Gam, &
K_1(\nabla\bt_1+\bI)\!\cdot\!\bn&=K_2(\nabla\bt_2+\bI)\!\cdot\!\bn \ \text{on }\Gam,
\label{eq:A-iface}
\end{align}
normalized by
$\int_{\Om_1}\bt_1\,d\Om+\int_{\Om_2}\bt_2\,d\Om=\mathbf 0$, and then
\begin{equation}
\Keff=\frac{1}{|\Om|}\left[\int_{\Om_1}\!K_1(\nabla\bt_1+\bI)\,d\Om
                          +\int_{\Om_2}\!K_2(\nabla\bt_2+\bI)\,d\Om\right].
\label{eq:A-keff}
\end{equation}
The two interface conditions are the physics: the field is continuous, and the
normal flux is conserved. This is Calonne et al.\ (2011), Eqs.~(7)--(11), whose
$\bt_i$ (ice) and $\bt_a$ (air) are our $\bt_1$ and $\bt_2$. In the jump
notation, \eqref{eq:A-iface} reads $\jump{\bt}=\mathbf 0$ and
$\jump{K(\nabla\bt+\bI)\!\cdot\!\bn}=\mathbf 0$, where the jump of a
two-field quantity means (side 1)$-$(side 2).
Note that A has two unknowns, B below has one; reconciling that is part of the
claim (\S\ref{sec:step1}(f)).

\paragraph{Problem B (phase field).} Find cell-periodic $\bt$ with
\begin{equation}
\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(K(\varphi)(\nabla\bt+\bI)\big)=\mathbf 0 \ \text{in }\Om,
\qquad \avg{\bt}=\mathbf 0,
\qquad K(\varphi)=K_2+(K_1-K_2)\varphi,
\label{eq:B}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\Keff=\frac{1}{|\Om|}\int_{\Om} K(\varphi)(\nabla\bt+\bI)\,d\Om .
\label{eq:B-keff}
\end{equation}
One domain, one equation, no interface conditions.

\paragraph{Claim.} A and B are the same problem. Two steps:
\S\ref{sec:step1} collapses A's two domains and two correctors into one weak
equation for one field;
\S\ref{sec:step2} shows A's coefficient \emph{is} B's coefficient. Neither step
is a limit.

\section{Step 1: two domains and two correctors become one weak equation}
\label{sec:step1}

Let $v\in\Hper$ be an arbitrary cell-periodic scalar test function. We treat
each subdomain separately, then add.

\paragraph{(a) Test each bulk equation on its own subdomain.}
Multiply the first equation of \eqref{eq:A-bulk} by $v$ and integrate over
$\Om_1$; do the same on $\Om_2$:
\begin{equation}
\int_{\Om_1}\! v\,\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(K_1(\nabla\bt_1+\bI)\big)\,d\Om=0,
\qquad
\int_{\Om_2}\! v\,\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(K_2(\nabla\bt_2+\bI)\big)\,d\Om=0 .
\label{eq:s1-multiply}
\end{equation}
Both hold because the integrands vanish pointwise. No approximation yet.

\paragraph{(b) Integrate by parts on each subdomain.}
Using $\int_D v\,\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf A=-\int_D\nabla v\cdot\mathbf A
+\oint_{\partial D}v\,\mathbf A\!\cdot\!\bn_D$ with $\bn_D$ the outward normal
of $D$. The boundary of $\Om_1$ consists of $\Gam$, where its outward normal is
$+\bn$, and whatever part of the outer cell boundary $\partial\Om$ it touches;
likewise for $\Om_2$, whose outward normal on $\Gam$ is $-\bn$. Writing
$\mathbf A_m=K_m(\nabla\bt_m+\bI)$ for $m=1,2$,
\begin{align}
0&=-\int_{\Om_1}\!\nabla v\cdot\mathbf A_1\,d\Om
   \;+\;\int_{\Gam} v\,\mathbf A_1\!\cdot\!\bn\,d\Gam
   \;+\;\underbrace{\int_{\partial\Om\cap\partial\Om_1}\!\! v\,\mathbf A_1\!\cdot\!\bn_{\partial\Om}\,dS}_{\textstyle O_1},
\label{eq:s1-parts1}\\
0&=-\int_{\Om_2}\!\nabla v\cdot\mathbf A_2\,d\Om
   \;-\;\int_{\Gam} v\,\mathbf A_2\!\cdot\!\bn\,d\Gam
   \;+\;\underbrace{\int_{\partial\Om\cap\partial\Om_2}\!\! v\,\mathbf A_2\!\cdot\!\bn_{\partial\Om}\,dS}_{\textstyle O_2}.
\label{eq:s1-parts2}
\end{align}
The sign flip on the $\Gam$ term of \eqref{eq:s1-parts2} is the only subtlety:
$\Gam$ is traversed with opposite orientation from the two sides.

\paragraph{(c) Add the two equations.}
This is the step that produces a single statement on $\Om$. Adding
\eqref{eq:s1-parts1} and \eqref{eq:s1-parts2},
\begin{equation}
0=-\left[\int_{\Om_1}\!\nabla v\cdot\mathbf A_1\,d\Om
        +\int_{\Om_2}\!\nabla v\cdot\mathbf A_2\,d\Om\right]
 +\int_{\Gam} v\,\big(\mathbf A_1-\mathbf A_2\big)\!\cdot\!\bn\,d\Gam
 +\;O_1+O_2 .
\label{eq:s1-added}
\end{equation}
Now dispose of the three groups in turn.

\paragraph{(d) The outer boundary terms cancel.}
$O_1+O_2$ is an integral over all of $\partial\Om$. Both $v$ and the fluxes
$\mathbf A_1,\mathbf A_2$ are cell-periodic, so they take equal values at
corresponding points of opposite faces, where $\bn_{\partial\Om}$ points in
opposite directions. Every contribution is therefore matched by its negative:
$O_1+O_2=0$.

\paragraph{(e) The interface term is exactly the flux jump, which vanishes.}
The middle term of \eqref{eq:s1-added} is
$\int_\Gam v\,(\mathbf A_1-\mathbf A_2)\!\cdot\!\bn\,d\Gam$, and
$(\mathbf A_1-\mathbf A_2)\!\cdot\!\bn=0$ on $\Gam$ is exactly the second
interface condition in \eqref{eq:A-iface}. So the term is $0$ for every $v$,
since the integrand vanishes pointwise.

\paragraph{(f) The two volume integrals merge into one.}
This is where $K_\star$ and the single corrector $\bt$ come from. For both it is
the same bookkeeping, with one extra check for $\bt$. Define the
piecewise-constant coefficient and the glued corrector
\begin{equation}
K_\star:=K_1\chi+K_2(1-\chi),
\qquad
\bt:=\bt_1\chi+\bt_2(1-\chi),
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
(K_\star,\bt)=
\begin{cases}(K_1,\bt_1) & \text{on }\Om_1,\\ (K_2,\bt_2) & \text{on }\Om_2 .\end{cases}
\label{eq:Kstar}
\end{equation}
Each piece of $\bt$ is only ever evaluated on its own subdomain, so $\bt_1$
never needs a value in $\Om_2$ or vice versa.

\emph{The coefficient.} $K_\star$ \emph{equals} $K_1$ at every point of $\Om_1$
and $K_2$ at every point of $\Om_2$. Nothing more is needed: a coefficient may
jump, and $K_\star$ does.

\emph{The corrector.} The extra check concerns the gradient. On each open
subdomain $\nabla\bt=\nabla\bt_m$, so the \emph{piecewise} gradient of $\bt$ is
$\chi\nabla\bt_1+(1-\chi)\nabla\bt_2$. The question is whether this is also the
gradient of $\bt$ \emph{as a function on $\Om$}, i.e.\ its weak gradient,
which is what a weak form on $\Om$ differentiates. Test against a smooth
periodic vector field $\boldsymbol\psi$ and integrate by parts on each
subdomain exactly as in (b), with the same orientations and the same periodic
cancellation as (d):
\begin{equation}
\int_\Om \bt\,\nabla\!\cdot\!\boldsymbol\psi\,d\Om
=-\int_{\Om_1}\!\nabla\bt_1\cdot\boldsymbol\psi\,d\Om
 -\int_{\Om_2}\!\nabla\bt_2\cdot\boldsymbol\psi\,d\Om
 +\int_\Gam\big(\bt_1-\bt_2\big)\,\boldsymbol\psi\!\cdot\!\bn\,d\Gam .
\label{eq:glue}
\end{equation}
The last term vanishes for every $\boldsymbol\psi$ \emph{if and only if}
$\bt_1=\bt_2$ on $\Gam$, the first interface condition in \eqref{eq:A-iface}.
When it does, $\bt\in\Hper$ and its weak gradient is the piecewise one:
\begin{equation}
\nabla\bt=\nabla\bt_1\ \text{on }\Om_1,\qquad \nabla\bt=\nabla\bt_2\ \text{on }\Om_2,
\qquad\text{with no part concentrated on }\Gam .
\label{eq:glue-grad}
\end{equation}
Had the fields jumped, $\nabla\bt$ would carry an extra surface term
$\jump{\bt}\,\bn\,\delta_\Gam$. The piecewise integrals of \eqref{eq:s1-added}
would not see it, but the weak form on $\Om$ would, so the two would no longer
agree. This is the only place where
$K_\star$ and $\bt$ behave differently. The coefficient may be discontinuous; the
corrector must not be, and continuity is exactly what permits the gluing.

\emph{The merge.} With \eqref{eq:Kstar} and \eqref{eq:glue-grad} we may
substitute on each subdomain without changing either integral:
\begin{equation}
\int_{\Om_1}\!\nabla v\cdot\mathbf A_1
+\int_{\Om_2}\!\nabla v\cdot\mathbf A_2
=\int_{\Om_1}\!\nabla v\cdot K_\star(\nabla\bt+\bI)
+\int_{\Om_2}\!\nabla v\cdot K_\star(\nabla\bt+\bI).
\label{eq:s1-sub}
\end{equation}
The two integrands are now the \emph{same function} of $\mathbf x$, integrated
over two disjoint sets whose union is $\Om$. Additivity of the integral gives
\begin{equation}
\int_{\Om_1}(\cdots)+\int_{\Om_2}(\cdots)=\int_{\Om_1\cup\Om_2}(\cdots)=\int_{\Om}(\cdots),
\label{eq:s1-merge}
\end{equation}
the last equality because $\Om_1\cup\Om_2$ differs from $\Om$ only by $\Gam$,
a set of measure zero. So neither $K_\star$ nor $\bt$ is assumed or fitted.
Each is the name for ``one thing on one piece and another on the other,'' and
\eqref{eq:s1-merge} is the only thing done with them. The same additivity turns
A's normalization into $\avg{\bt}=\mathbf 0$ and A's $\Keff$
\eqref{eq:A-keff} into $|\Om|^{-1}\!\int_\Om K_\star(\nabla\bt+\bI)$.

\paragraph{(g) Result.}
Substituting (d), (e), (f) into \eqref{eq:s1-added}:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\int_{\Om}\nabla v\cdot K_\star(\nabla\bt+\bI)\,d\Om=0
\qquad\forall\,v\in\Hper.\;}
\label{eq:weak}
\end{equation}

\begin{remark}[Where the two interface conditions went]
They were not dropped; each was absorbed somewhere.
\emph{Flux conservation} $\jump{K(\nabla\bt+\bI)\!\cdot\!\bn}=0$ was used in
step (e), and it is the \emph{natural} condition of \eqref{eq:weak}: run (e)
backwards and any weak solution satisfies it automatically.
\emph{Continuity} $\bt_1=\bt_2$ was used in step (f), to glue the two
correctors into one $\bt\in\Hper$ via \eqref{eq:glue}. Conversely, it is
built into \eqref{eq:weak}: a single $H^1$ field on $\Om$ cannot jump, so
restricting any weak solution to $\Om_1$ and $\Om_2$ returns a pair
$(\bt_1,\bt_2)$ with matching traces. Two correctors in A and one in B is
therefore not a difference between the problems. A's pair \emph{is} B's single
field, written in two pieces.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Why one arbitrary $v$ on $\Om$ is not an extra assumption]
It may look as though testing with a single $v$ defined on all of $\Om$ smuggles
in something. It does not, and the step reverses: given \eqref{eq:weak}, choose
$v$ supported strictly inside $\Om_1$. Then the integral is over $\Om_1$ only,
where $K_\star=K_1$ and $\bt=\bt_1$; undoing the integration by parts (no
boundary term, since $v$ vanishes near $\partial\Om_1$) returns the first
equation of \eqref{eq:A-bulk}. Symmetrically for $\Om_2$. Allowing $v$ to straddle $\Gam$
then returns the interface term, hence \eqref{eq:A-iface}. So \eqref{eq:weak} is
\emph{equivalent} to \eqref{eq:A-bulk}--\eqref{eq:A-iface}, not weaker.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Same algebra for the physical field]
Nothing in (a)--(g) used the specific form $\nabla\bt+\bI$. Replacing it by
$\nabla T$ throughout collapses the physical problem, with its own two
temperatures $T_1$ on $\Om_1$ and $T_2$ on $\Om_2$,
$\nabla\!\cdot\!(K_m\nabla T_m)=0$, $T_1=T_2$ and
$K_1\nabla T_1\!\cdot\!\bn=K_2\nabla T_2\!\cdot\!\bn$ on $\Gam$, into
$\int_\Om\nabla v\cdot K_\star\nabla T=0$ for the glued $T$, identically.
\end{remark}

\section{Step 2: the coefficient is already the phase-field interpolation}
\label{sec:step2}

It is tempting to finish by saying ``now replace $K_\star$ by a smooth $K(\varphi)$
that converges to it.'' That would be an approximation, and would need a
convergence theorem. No replacement is needed.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:identity}
Let $K(\cdot)$ be \emph{any} function with $K(0)=K_2$ and $K(1)=K_1$. Then
\begin{equation}
K(\chi)=K_\star \qquad\text{pointwise on }\Om,
\label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
exactly --- no limit, no smallness of $\varepsilon$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$\chi$ takes only the values $0$ and $1$. At $\mathbf x\in\Om_1$, $\chi=1$, so
$K(\chi)=K(1)=K_1=K_\star$. At $\mathbf x\in\Om_2$, $\chi=0$, so
$K(\chi)=K(0)=K_2=K_\star$. Those are all the points of $\Om$.
\end{proof}

\noindent
For the affine $K$ of \eqref{eq:B} it is three lines:
\begin{equation}
K_\star \overset{\eqref{eq:Kstar}}{=} K_1\chi+K_2(1-\chi)=K_2+(K_1-K_2)\chi=K(\chi).
\label{eq:algebra}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{What this means.} Problems A and B do not have two constitutive laws
that agree asymptotically. They have \emph{one} law $K(\cdot)$, evaluated at two
different arguments:
\[
\text{A: }K(\chi),\qquad\qquad \text{B: }K(\varphi).
\]
So Step 2 approximates the \emph{geometry}, never the equations, and every
difference between A and B traces to the single object $\varphi-\chi$. A second
consequence: since $\chi$ never takes an intermediate value, the \emph{shape} of
$K$ between $0$ and $1$ is invisible to Problem A. Every admissible
interpolation --- arithmetic, harmonic, any other --- coincides exactly on
$\chi$. The choice can therefore matter only through the diffuse band
(\S\ref{sec:excess}).

\begin{remark}[Why the corrector needs no Step 2]
\label{rem:no-tphi}
$K_\star$ and $\bt$ are glued the same way in (f), but only $K_\star$ reappears
here. There is no ``$\bt(\varphi)$'' interpolating $\bt_1$ and $\bt_2$ across
the band, and none is needed. $K$ is \emph{data}: B has to be told what
coefficient to use at every point, including inside the band, and the lemma
shows its prescription $K(\varphi)$ reduces to A's on $\chi$. $\bt$ is the
\emph{unknown}: B solves for one field on all of $\Om$, and inside the band it
simply takes whatever values the equation produces. At finite $\varepsilon$
the two sharp pieces $\bt_1,\bt_2$ have no separate meaning inside $\Band$, and
nothing requires them to. After Step~1 A also has a single unknown in the same
space $\Hper$, so the two problems differ only in their coefficient, and that
difference is carried entirely by $\varphi-\chi$.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:exact}
If $\varphi=\chi$, Problems A and B are the same boundary-value problem and give
the same $\Keff$, identically.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Step 1 gives A $\Leftrightarrow$ \eqref{eq:weak}. Lemma~\ref{lem:identity} gives
$K_\star=K(\chi)$, so \eqref{eq:weak} is the weak form of \eqref{eq:B}. The two
$\Keff$ formulas agree by \eqref{eq:s1-sub}--\eqref{eq:s1-merge} applied to the
integrand $K(\nabla\bt+\bI)$.
\end{proof}

\section{Finite $\varepsilon$: what is left over}
\label{sec:diff}

In practice $\varphi\neq\chi$, so Proposition~\ref{prop:exact} does not apply
verbatim. By Lemma~\ref{lem:identity} the entire residual difference is carried
by $\varphi-\chi$. For the affine $K$ this is exact:
\begin{equation}
\delta\!K:=K(\varphi)-K_\star=(K_1-K_2)(\varphi-\chi),
\qquad \operatorname{supp}\delta\!K=\Band:=\{0<\varphi<1\}.
\label{eq:dK}
\end{equation}
Both problems are uniformly elliptic on the same bounds, $\min(K_1,K_2)\le K\le
\max(K_1,K_2)$, so both are well posed and both admit the Dirichlet minimum
principle: for a unit direction $\be$,
\begin{equation}
A(K):=\be\cdot\Keff[K]\,\be=\min_{v\in\Hper}E_K[v],
\qquad E_K[v]:=\avg{(\nabla v+\be)\cdot K(\nabla v+\be)}.
\label{eq:dirichlet}
\end{equation}
Write $K_1^{\phantom{.}}\!\to K(\varphi)$, $K_2^{\phantom{.}}\!\to K_\star$ with
minimizers $v_\varepsilon,v_\star$. Here $v_\star$ is the \emph{glued} sharp
corrector of \eqref{eq:Kstar}; it is because gluing puts it in the same space
$\Hper$ as $v_\varepsilon$ that each can be tested in the other's energy. For
\emph{any} $v$,
$E_{K(\varphi)}[v]-E_{K_\star}[v]=\avg{\delta\!K|\nabla v+\be|^2}$. Testing each
minimizer against the other's energy gives two inequalities which combine into
\begin{equation}
\avg{\delta\!K\,|\nabla v_\varepsilon+\be|^2}
\;\le\;A_\varepsilon-A_\star\;\le\;
\avg{\delta\!K\,|\nabla v_\star+\be|^2}.
\label{eq:sandwich}
\end{equation}
Both sides are integrals over $\Band$ only. The left one uses only the corrector
already computed, so \eqref{eq:sandwich} is an \emph{a posteriori} error bar, not
an asymptotic claim. With the logistic profile
$\varphi=\ell(s/\varepsilon)$, $\ell(u)=(1+e^{-u})^{-1}$ (the logistic
function, unrelated to the excesses $\Sigma$ of \S\ref{sec:excess}), in the signed
distance $s$ to $\Gam$, and $\int_0^\infty(1-\ell)\,du=\ln2$,
\begin{equation}
\|\varphi-\chi\|_{L^1(\Om)}=2\ln\!2\;\varepsilon\,|\Gam|+\mathcal O(\varepsilon^2)
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\big|A_\varepsilon-A_\star\big|\le
2\ln\!2\,(K_1-K_2)\,M^2\,\frac{|\Gam|}{|\Om|}\,\varepsilon+\mathcal O(\varepsilon^2),
\label{eq:rate}
\end{equation}
with $M^2=\max_{\Band}|\nabla v+\be|^2$. First order, computable prefactor, and
no H-convergence theorem required. (That theorem is the right citation only for
a non-affine $K$, where \eqref{eq:dK} becomes an inequality.)

\section{The first-order error is a surface excess}
\label{sec:excess}

To first order a thin graded layer is equivalent to a sharp interface carrying
two \emph{surface excesses} per unit area. Transport tangential to $\Gam$ crosses
the layer's sublayers in parallel and sees an excess conductance; transport
normal to it crosses them in series and sees an excess resistance:
\begin{equation}
\Sigma_t=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\big[K(\varphi)-K_\star\big]ds,
\qquad
\Sigma_n=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\left[\frac{1}{K(\varphi)}-\frac{1}{K_\star}\right]ds.
\label{eq:excess}
\end{equation}
These two numbers are the only $\mathcal O(\varepsilon)$ data the band presents.
Both are elementary for the logistic profile, whose defining property here is
the antisymmetry $\ell(-u)=1-\ell(u)$.

\paragraph{$\Sigma_t=0$, exactly.} $K$ is affine, so the integrand is
$(K_1-K_2)[\ell(u)-H(u)]$ with $H$ the unit step, and antisymmetry makes
$\ell-H$ an odd function:
\begin{equation}
\Sigma_t=(K_1-K_2)\,\varepsilon\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\big[\ell(u)-H(u)\big]du=0 .
\label{eq:sigmat}
\end{equation}
Not first order --- zero. The diffuse cell has \emph{exactly} the sharp cell's
phase fraction, so the parallel response carries no error at any $\varepsilon$.
The leading correction is $\mathcal O(\varepsilon^2\kappa)$ from interface
curvature.

\paragraph{$\Sigma_n\neq0$.} Here $1/K$ is convex in $\varphi$, so the band
short-circuits a series resistance. With $p=\ell(u)$, $du=dp/[p(1-p)]$ and
partial fractions,
\begin{equation}
\Sigma_n=\varepsilon\!\int_0^1\!\left[\frac{1}{K_2+(K_1-K_2)p}-\frac{1}{K_\star}\right]\frac{dp}{p(1-p)}
=-\,\varepsilon\left(\frac{1}{K_2}-\frac{1}{K_1}\right)\ln\frac{K_1}{K_2}\;<\;0 .
\label{eq:sigman}
\end{equation}
Negative excess resistance: the diffuse cell conducts too well normal to $\Gam$,
first order in $\varepsilon$, with no free constants. This is the quantitative
form of the familiar statement that an arithmetic mixture rule is right in
parallel and wrong in series.

\begin{corollary}[An interpolation with no first-order bias]
\label{cor:tensor}
The harmonic law $K_{\mathrm h}(\varphi)=\big(\varphi/K_1+(1-\varphi)/K_2\big)^{-1}$
has $1/K_{\mathrm h}$ affine in $\varphi$, so the same antisymmetry gives
$\Sigma_n=0$ --- but then $\Sigma_t\neq0$. No scalar law nulls both. The
tensorial law
\begin{equation}
\mathbf K(\varphi)=K(\varphi)\big(\bI-\bn\otimes\bn\big)
+K_{\mathrm h}(\varphi)\,\bn\otimes\bn,
\qquad \bn=\nabla\varphi/|\nabla\varphi|,
\label{eq:tensorK}
\end{equation}
uses each where its excess vanishes, giving $\Sigma_t=\Sigma_n=0$ and
$\mathcal O(\varepsilon^2)$. Both branches satisfy $K(0)=K_2$, $K(1)=K_1$, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:identity} and Proposition~\ref{prop:exact} still apply.
\end{corollary}

\noindent
This is available because the conductivity cell problem is post-processing on a
frozen $\varphi$: its interpolation is independent of any other model, and
choosing it does not bear on one. \S\ref{sec:steady} applies it to the
cell problem.

\section{Worked example and test: the laminate}
\label{sec:laminate}

For layers of period $L$ with phase fraction $\bar\varphi$ and $n_\Gam$
interfaces per period, $\Keff$ is diagonal with $k_\parallel=\avg{K}$ and
$k_\perp=\avg{K^{-1}}^{-1}$. Equations \eqref{eq:sigmat}--\eqref{eq:sigman} give
\begin{align}
k_\parallel(\varepsilon)&=K_2+(K_1-K_2)\bar\varphi
&&\text{exact for every }\varepsilon,
\label{eq:lampar}\\
\avg{K^{-1}}_\varepsilon&=\frac{\bar\varphi}{K_1}+\frac{1-\bar\varphi}{K_2}
-\frac{n_\Gam\varepsilon}{L}\left(\frac{1}{K_2}-\frac{1}{K_1}\right)\ln\frac{K_1}{K_2},
&&k_\perp=\avg{K^{-1}}_\varepsilon^{-1}.
\label{eq:lamperp}
\end{align}
Equation \eqref{eq:lamperp} is not a truncation: its only error is tail overlap
between neighbouring interfaces, $\mathcal O(e^{-L/4\varepsilon})$, which against
direct quadrature is $5\times10^{-5}$ relative at $\varepsilon=L/50$ and machine
precision by $\varepsilon=L/128$.

In our application $K_1/K_2=2.29/0.02=114.5$, giving
$(K_2^{-1}-K_1^{-1})\ln(K_1/K_2)=234.959$ and, at $\bar\varphi=\tfrac12$,
$n_\Gam=2$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
$\varepsilon$ & $\avg{K^{-1}}$ & $k_\perp$ & bias \\
\midrule
$L/50$  & 15.8200 & 0.063211 & $+59.4\%$ \\
$L/128$ & 21.5471 & 0.046410 & $+17.0\%$ \\
$L/512$ & 24.3005 & 0.041151 & $+3.8\%$ \\
sharp   & 25.2183 & 0.039654 & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{How to test it.} Equation \eqref{eq:lamperp} is \emph{linear in
$\varepsilon$ in resistivity} and a hyperbola in conductivity. Fit and plot
$1/k_\perp$ against $\varepsilon$; then the test has two predicted numbers and no
fitted ones,
\begin{equation}
\text{intercept}=\frac{\bar\varphi}{K_1}+\frac{1-\bar\varphi}{K_2},
\qquad
\text{slope}=\frac{n_\Gam}{L}\left(\frac{1}{K_2}-\frac{1}{K_1}\right)\ln\frac{K_1}{K_2}.
\label{eq:fit}
\end{equation}
Two ways to get a meaningless answer: evaluating \eqref{eq:fit} at the nominal
rather than the \emph{measured} $\bar\varphi$ (the discrete field's mean is not
exactly $\bar\varphi$), and refining $\varepsilon$ at fixed mesh, which
confounds this bias with mesh convergence. The committed driver is
\verb|studies/keff_sharp_limit/verification/|.

\section{Applying the tensor law to the steady-state cell problem}
\label{sec:steady}

\paragraph{Scope.} Everything in this note concerns the \emph{steady-state}
corrector problem that defines $\Keff$ for a frozen microstructure. It says
nothing about the transient energy balance of the metamorphism models, which
have their own interpolations. Nothing here justifies, or asks for, a change to
them.

\paragraph{Strong form.} Replace the scalar coefficient of \eqref{eq:B} by the
tensor \eqref{eq:tensorK}, written $\mathbf K$ below with $K(\varphi)$ the
arithmetic law. For each direction $m$, find cell-periodic $t_m$ with
\begin{equation}
\nabla\!\cdot\!\Big(\mathbf K(\varphi,\nabla\varphi)\,\big(\nabla t_m+\be_m\big)\Big)=0
\ \text{in }\Om,
\qquad \avg{t_m}=0,
\qquad
\Keff\,\be_m=\avg{\mathbf K\,(\nabla t_m+\be_m)} .
\label{eq:steady-strong}
\end{equation}
With $\mathbf G=\nabla t_m+\be_m$, the local flux splits into a part along the
interface and a part across it,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf K\mathbf G=K(\varphi)\,\mathbf G
+\big(K_{\mathrm h}(\varphi)-K(\varphi)\big)\,(\bn\cdot\mathbf G)\,\bn ,
\label{eq:flux-split}
\end{equation}
so only the normal component of the gradient sees the harmonic mean. In the bulk,
where $\varphi\in\{0,1\}$, the correction term vanishes and
\eqref{eq:steady-strong} is the scalar problem of Proposition~\ref{prop:exact}.

\paragraph{Weak form.} Multiply by a periodic test function $v$ and integrate
by parts. Periodicity removes the boundary term exactly as in step (d):
\begin{equation}
\int_\Om\nabla v\cdot\mathbf K\,\nabla t_m\,d\Om
=-\int_\Om\nabla v\cdot\mathbf K\,\be_m\,d\Om
\qquad\forall\,v\in\Hper .
\label{eq:steady-weak}
\end{equation}
The bilinear form on the left does not depend on $m$; only the load does.

\paragraph{Well posed, with the same bounds.} $\mathbf K$ is symmetric, with
eigenvalue $K(\varphi)$ on the tangent plane and $K_{\mathrm h}(\varphi)$ along
$\bn$. Both lie in $[\min(K_1,K_2),\max(K_1,K_2)]$, so
\begin{equation}
\min(K_1,K_2)\,|\boldsymbol\xi|^2\le\boldsymbol\xi\cdot\mathbf K\,\boldsymbol\xi
\le\max(K_1,K_2)\,|\boldsymbol\xi|^2 .
\end{equation}
By Lax--Milgram, \eqref{eq:steady-weak} has a unique solution with
$\avg{t_m}=0$. The minimum principle \eqref{eq:dirichlet} holds with $K$
replaced by $\mathbf K$, and so does the sandwich \eqref{eq:sandwich}. Testing
\eqref{eq:steady-weak} for direction $i$ with $v=t_j$ gives the symmetric energy
form
\begin{equation}
\be_i\cdot\Keff\,\be_j=\avg{(\nabla t_i+\be_i)\cdot\mathbf K\,(\nabla t_j+\be_j)},
\end{equation}
so $\Keff$ is symmetric positive definite, as it must be.

\paragraph{Discrete form.} With basis functions $N_a$ and
$t_m=\sum_b T_{m,b}N_b$, \eqref{eq:steady-weak} becomes
\begin{equation}
\mathsf A\,\mathsf T_m=\mathsf F_m,\qquad
\mathsf A_{ab}=\int_\Om\nabla N_a\cdot\mathbf K\,\nabla N_b\,d\Om,\qquad
(\mathsf F_m)_a=-\int_\Om\nabla N_a\cdot\mathbf K\,\be_m\,d\Om .
\label{eq:discrete}
\end{equation}
$\mathsf A$ is symmetric positive semidefinite, with the constants as its only
null space (removed by the mean constraint). It is assembled once and reused for
all directions, and conjugate gradients with algebraic multigrid apply as in the
scalar case. $\mathbf K$ is evaluated at each quadrature point from $\varphi$
and $\nabla\varphi$ there.

\paragraph{Worked example: the laminate.} Here $\bn=\pm\be_y$ everywhere, so
$\mathbf K=\mathrm{diag}\big(K(\varphi),K_{\mathrm h}(\varphi)\big)$ and
\eqref{eq:steady-strong} decouples. Along the layers the corrector is zero and
$k_\parallel=\avg{K(\varphi)}$. Across them the flux is constant, so
$k_\perp=\avg{1/K_{\mathrm h}(\varphi)}^{-1}$. Both averages are of functions
affine in $\varphi$, so both equal the sharp values at every $\varepsilon$
(up to the $\mathcal O(e^{-L/4\varepsilon})$ tail overlap of
\S\ref{sec:laminate}):
\begin{equation}
k_\parallel=K_2+(K_1-K_2)\bar\varphi,\qquad
k_\perp=\Big(\frac{\bar\varphi}{K_1}+\frac{1-\bar\varphi}{K_2}\Big)^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Compare \eqref{eq:lamperp}: the $\varepsilon$ term is gone. Measured, the
tensor law reproduces $k_\perp$ to $4\times10^{-7}$ from $\varepsilon=L/50$ to
$L/512$, where the scalar law is $59\%$ to $3.8\%$ high. The derivation of
\eqref{eq:tensorK} and a curved-interface test (a disk array against Rayleigh's
solution) are in the companion writeup
\verb|tensor_conductivity_law.tex|.

\section{Where this lives in the code}
\label{sec:code}

The cell problem is solved in-line by \verb|enceladus_dsm| (\verb|-keff 1|),
or on saved snapshots with \verb|-keff_replay|. The law is chosen with
\verb|-keff_interp arith|$\,|\,$\verb|tensor|$\,|\,$\verb|sharp|; the default is
\verb|tensor| (before 2026-09-23 it was \verb|arith|). Non-default laws write
\verb|k_eff_<law>.csv|.

\paragraph{Inputs at each quadrature point}
(\verb|src/keff_field.c|, \verb|KeffProjectIce|). The phase field and its
gradient are evaluated on the solver mesh and stored per quadrature point:
\verb|kc->ice[idx]| $=\varphi$ and \verb|kc->grad_ice[idx*dim+d]|
$=\partial_d\varphi$. The gradient array is allocated only for the tensor law.

\paragraph{The coefficient} (\verb|src/keff_cell.c|, \verb|KeffPointCond|),
row-major $\mathbf K$ = \verb|K[a*dim+b]|:
\begin{lstlisting}
ThermalCond(kc->app, phi, &cond, NULL);           /* K(phi), arithmetic   */
for (a = 0; a < dim; a++) K[a*dim + a] = cond;    /* K(phi) I             */
if (kc->interp == KEFF_INTERP_TENSOR) {
  k_harm = 1.0 / (phi/k_ice + (1.0 - phi)/k_air); /* K_h(phi)             */
  g2 = |grad phi|^2;
  if (g2 > 0.0)                                   /* + (K_h - K) n (x) n  */
    for (a...) for (b...)
      K[a*dim + b] += (k_harm - cond) * g[a]*g[b] / g2;
}
\end{lstlisting}
This is \eqref{eq:tensorK} written as \eqref{eq:flux-split}:
$K\,\bI+(K_{\mathrm h}-K)\,\bn\otimes\bn$ with
$\bn\otimes\bn=\nabla\varphi\otimes\nabla\varphi/|\nabla\varphi|^2$, so $\bn$ is
never formed explicitly. Where $\nabla\varphi=0$ the correction is skipped;
that happens only in the bulk, where $K_{\mathrm h}=K$. $\varphi$ is first
clamped to $[0,1]$ so both branches see the same admissible value.

\paragraph{The three integrals of \eqref{eq:discrete} and $\Keff$.}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
routine & integrand accumulated & equation\\
\midrule
\verb|KeffFormMatrix| & $\nabla N_a\cdot\mathbf K\,\nabla N_b$ & $\mathsf A_{ab}$\\
\verb|KeffFormVector| & $-\nabla N_a\cdot(\text{column }m\text{ of }\mathbf K)$ & $(\mathsf F_m)_a$\\
\verb|KeffScalarIntegrand| & $\big[\mathbf K(\nabla t_m+\be_m)\big]_j$ & row $m$ of $\Keff\,|\Om|$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
PetIGA applies the quadrature weight and Jacobian, and \verb|KeffComputeTensor|
divides by the cell volume measured by the same quadrature. For the isotropic
laws \verb|KeffPointCond| returns $K\bI$, and the three routines reduce exactly
to the scalar assembly.
