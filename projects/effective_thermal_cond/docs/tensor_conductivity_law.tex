\begin{center}
{\large\bfseries A tensor conductivity law that removes the first-order\\[2pt]
diffuse-interface bias from homogenized $\Keff$}
\end{center}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent We compute the effective thermal conductivity $\Keff$ of evolving snow
microstructures by solving the steady-state homogenization cell problem of
Calonne et al.\ (2011) directly on the phase field, where the ice--air
interface is a diffuse band a few $\varepsilon$ wide rather than a sharp
surface. Inside that band the conductivity must be interpolated, and the
standard arithmetic mixture law lets the band's sub-layers conduct in parallel
in every direction, although across the interface they are stacked in series;
the band therefore short-circuits the interface's thermal resistance, with an
error proportional to $\varepsilon$. At $K_{\mathrm i}/K_{\mathrm a}\approx115$
this error is large: in the worst case, heat flowing across a layered cell with
$\varepsilon=L/50$, the computed conductivity is
$0.063$~W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$ where the exact value for the same geometry is
$0.040$, $59\%$ too high only because the interface has a numerical width. And
since the error scales with $\varepsilon$ times the interface area per unit
volume, it changes as sintering reduces that area, so it distorts the time
trend of $\Keff$, not just its level. We fix it by giving the band a tensor
conductivity, arithmetic along the interface and harmonic across it. This is
the exact conductivity of a fine ice--air laminate lying parallel to the
interface, and it enforces inside the band the same two conditions a sharp
interface imposes (temperature continuous along it, heat flux continuous
through it), so it cancels both first-order error terms with no fitted
parameter, while leaving the bulk phases, the sharp limit and the metamorphism
model untouched. Against closed-form sharp solutions it reproduces a laminate's
conductivity to $4\times10^{-7}$ at every interface width tested, where the
arithmetic law is $3.8$--$59\%$ high, and a disk array's to $0.03\%$ at
$\varepsilon=L/800$ with the error falling at second order in $\varepsilon$,
while the arithmetic law's measured error matches our first-order prediction to
within $1\%$.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem}

The effective conductivity of a periodic ice/air cell is obtained from the
corrector problem
\begin{equation}
\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(\bK(\nabla t_m+\be_m)\big)=0\ \text{in }\Om,\qquad
t_m\ \text{periodic},\qquad
\Keff\,\be_m=\avg{\bK(\nabla t_m+\be_m)} ,
\label{eq:cell}
\end{equation}
for each direction $m$. With a sharp interface, $\bK=K_\star\bI$, where
$K_\star=\Ki$ in ice and $\Ka$ in air; this is the problem of Calonne et al.\
(2011). With a phase field $\varphi$ the coefficient must also be defined
\emph{inside} the diffuse band, and the usual choice is the arithmetic law
$\Kar(\varphi)=\Ka+(\Ki-\Ka)\varphi$. The two problems coincide exactly when
$\varphi$ is the indicator function of the ice
(companion note, Prop.~1). At finite $\varepsilon$ they differ, by an amount
of order $\varepsilon$ that depends on the interpolation law. For
$\Ki/\Ka=114.5$ this bias is large: $+17\%$ at $\varepsilon=L/128$ for a
laminate loaded normal to its layers.

The interpolation is invisible to the sharp problem, since every law with
$K(0)=\Ka$, $K(1)=\Ki$ agrees on the indicator. It is therefore free to choose,
and we choose it to cancel the first-order bias.

\section{Structure of the diffuse band}

Let $s$ be the signed distance to the interface $\Gam$ (the $\varphi=\tfrac12$
level set), positive in ice, and let the profile be logistic,
\begin{equation}
\varphi=\ell(s/\varepsilon),\qquad \ell(u)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-u}},\qquad
\ell(-u)=1-\ell(u).
\label{eq:profile}
\end{equation}
where $\ell$ is the logistic function. It only describes the shape of the
profile, and is unrelated to the excesses $\Sigma_t$, $\Sigma_n$ defined in
\S3. When $\varepsilon$ is small compared with the interface's radius of curvature
and with the spacing between interfaces, the band is locally a flat
one-dimensional layer. Write the temperature gradient $\mathbf E=\nabla T$ and
the flux $\mathbf q=\bK\mathbf E$. At leading order in the stretched coordinate
$u=s/\varepsilon$, two quantities are constant across the layer:
\begin{itemize}
\item the \emph{tangential gradient} $\mathbf E_t$, because $\mathbf E$ is a
gradient (curl-free), so its tangential part cannot vary over the short normal
distance $O(\varepsilon)$;
\item the \emph{normal flux} $q_n=\mathbf q\cdot\bn$, because
$\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf q=0$ and the tangential flux divergence is $O(1)$ while
$\partial_s$ is $O(\varepsilon^{-1})$.
\end{itemize}
These are exactly the quantities that the sharp interface conditions make
continuous: the temperature (hence $\mathbf E_t$) and the normal flux. Inside
the band, then, the tangential sub-layers act in \emph{parallel} and the normal
ones in \emph{series}.

\section{Surface excesses and the first-order bias}

Because the band acts in parallel along $\Gam$ and in series across it, it is
equivalent, to first order, to a sharp interface carrying two excesses per unit
area:
\begin{equation}
\Sigma_t=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\big[K_t(\varphi)-K_\star\big]\,ds,
\qquad
\Sigma_n=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\left[\frac{1}{K_n(\varphi)}-\frac{1}{K_\star}\right]ds,
\label{eq:excess}
\end{equation}
where $K_t$ and $K_n$ are the band's conductivities along and across the
interface ($K_t=K_n=K$ for a scalar law). $\Sigma_t$ is an excess
\emph{conductance}, $\Sigma_n$ an excess \emph{resistance}. This is the
imperfect-interface (thin-interphase) limit of Hashin (2001) and Benveniste
(2006a, 2006b).

\emph{What the two numbers mean.} Take a patch of interface of unit area and
follow a line straight through the band. Along the interface the sub-layers
conduct side by side (in parallel), so the patch's conductance per unit length
is $\int K_t\,ds$; a sharp interface would give $\int K_\star\,ds$. $\Sigma_t$ is
the difference: the conductance the diffuse band adds, or removes if negative,
compared with a sharp interface. Across the interface the sub-layers are stacked
(in series), so what adds is resistance, $\int 1/K_n\,ds$, and $\Sigma_n$ is the
resistance the band adds compared with a sharp interface. Both integrands vanish
outside the band, so both integrals are finite. They are the conduction analogue
of Gibbs surface excesses.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:bias}
For a unit mean gradient along $\be$,
\begin{equation}
\be\cdot\Keff^{\varepsilon}\be-\be\cdot\Keff^{\star}\be
=\frac{1}{|\Om|}\int_\Gam\Big(\Sigma_t\,|\mathbf E_t|^2-\Sigma_n\,q_n^2\Big)\,d\Gam
+O(\varepsilon^2),
\label{eq:bias}
\end{equation}
with $\mathbf E_t$ and $q_n$ taken from the \emph{sharp} solution.
\end{proposition}

\noindent\emph{Justification.} $\be\cdot\Keff\be$ is a minimum of the energy
$\avg{\mathbf E\cdot\bK\mathbf E}$ (Dirichlet principle) and, dually, a maximum
in terms of the flux (Thomson principle). Split the band's energy density as
$K_tE_t^2+q_n^2/K_n$. A change in tangential conductance at fixed $\mathbf E_t$
alters the energy by $\Sigma_t E_t^2$. A change in normal resistance at fixed
$q_n$ alters the complementary energy by $\Sigma_n q_n^2$, which changes the
conductance by $-\Sigma_n q_n^2$. By stationarity, the accompanying change in
the field enters only at second order. The companion note (Eq.~21) gives a
rigorous two-sided bound of the same quantity that does not rely on the
expansion.

\emph{Check (laminate).} For layers of period $L$ with $n_\Gam$ interfaces, a
gradient normal to the layers has $\mathbf E_t=0$ and $q_n=k_\perp$, and
\eqref{eq:bias} gives $\delta\avg{K^{-1}}=\Sigma_n n_\Gam/L$, exactly the
closed form. A gradient along the layers has $q_n=0$, $|\mathbf E_t|=1$, and
gives $\delta k_\parallel=\Sigma_t n_\Gam/L$.

\section{Excesses of the scalar laws}
\label{sec:excess-calc}

This section evaluates the two integrals \eqref{eq:excess} for a scalar law
$K(\varphi)$, in four steps.

\paragraph{Step 1: every excess is proportional to $\varepsilon$.} Across the
band, the diffuse coefficient is $K(\ell(u))$ and the sharp one is $K(H(u))$,
with $u=s/\varepsilon$ and $H$ the unit step ($H=1$ in ice, $0$ in air). This
is Lemma~1 of the companion note: the sharp coefficient is the same law
evaluated at the indicator. Changing variables $s=\varepsilon u$ in
\eqref{eq:excess} gives
\begin{equation}
\Sigma_t=\varepsilon\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\big[K(\ell(u))-K(H(u))\big]\,du,
\qquad
\Sigma_n=\varepsilon\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\left[\frac{1}{K(\ell(u))}-\frac{1}{K(H(u))}\right]du .
\label{eq:excess-u}
\end{equation}
The integrals are pure numbers set by $K_{\mathrm i}/K_{\mathrm a}$ alone. So each
excess is $\varepsilon$ times a constant, and by \eqref{eq:bias} the bias in
$\Keff$ is first order in $\varepsilon$ unless that constant is zero.

\paragraph{Step 2: a quantity affine in $\varphi$ has zero excess.} Suppose the
integrand is $f(\varphi)=\alpha+\beta\varphi$. Then
$f(\ell(u))-f(H(u))=\beta\,[\ell(u)-H(u)]$, and $\ell-H$ is an \emph{odd}
function. For $u>0$, $\ell(u)-H(u)=\ell(u)-1=-\ell(-u)$ by the antisymmetry in
\eqref{eq:profile}, and $\ell(-u)-H(-u)=\ell(-u)$. So the positive lobe on the
air side and the negative lobe on the ice side have equal area
(Fig.~\ref{fig:lobes}), and
\begin{equation}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big[\ell(u)-H(u)\big]\,du=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{excess of }f=0 .
\label{eq:odd}
\end{equation}
Physically, a diffuse band that is exactly antisymmetric about $\varphi=\tfrac12$
adds as much ice on the air side as it removes on the ice side.

\noindent Apply this to the two laws.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Arithmetic}, $K_{\mathrm{ar}}=K_{\mathrm a}+(K_{\mathrm i}-K_{\mathrm a})\varphi$, is affine,
so $\Sigma_t=0$. But $1/K_{\mathrm{ar}}$ is not affine, so $\Sigma_n$ must be computed.
\item \emph{Harmonic},
$1/K_{\mathrm h}=\varphi/K_{\mathrm i}+(1-\varphi)/K_{\mathrm a}$, is affine,
so $\Sigma_n=0$. But $K_{\mathrm h}$ is not affine, so $\Sigma_t$ must be
computed.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Step 3: computing the nonzero excess.} Take $\Sigma_n$ for the
arithmetic law; write $a=K_{\mathrm a}$, $b=K_{\mathrm i}$, $c=b-a$, so
$K(p)=a+cp$. Substitute $p=\ell(u)$. Since $\ell'(u)=\ell(1-\ell)$,
$du=dp/[p(1-p)]$, and $u\gtrless0$ becomes $p\gtrless\tfrac12$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\Sigma_n}{\varepsilon}
=\int_0^{1/2}\!\Big[\frac{1}{K(p)}-\frac1a\Big]\frac{dp}{p(1-p)}
+\int_{1/2}^{1}\!\Big[\frac{1}{K(p)}-\frac1b\Big]\frac{dp}{p(1-p)} .
\end{equation}
Each bracket vanishes at its own endpoint, which cancels one factor of the
denominator:
$\frac1{K}-\frac1a=-\frac{cp}{aK}$ and $\frac1K-\frac1b=\frac{c(1-p)}{bK}$. So
\begin{equation}
\frac{\Sigma_n}{\varepsilon}
=-\frac{c}{a}\int_0^{1/2}\frac{dp}{K(p)\,(1-p)}
+\frac{c}{b}\int_{1/2}^{1}\frac{dp}{K(p)\,p} .
\end{equation}
Partial fractions,
$\frac{1}{K(1-p)}=\frac1b\big[\frac{c}{K}+\frac{1}{1-p}\big]$ and
$\frac{1}{Kp}=\frac1a\big[\frac1p-\frac{c}{K}\big]$, give elementary logarithms.
With $K_m=K(\tfrac12)$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\Sigma_n}{\varepsilon}
=\frac{c}{ab}\Big[-\ln\frac{K_m}{a}-\ln2\;+\;\ln2-\ln\frac{b}{K_m}\Big]
=-\frac{c}{ab}\ln\frac ba ,
\end{equation}
that is,
\begin{equation}
\Sigma_n^{\mathrm{ar}}=-\varepsilon\Big(\frac1{K_{\mathrm a}}-\frac1{K_{\mathrm i}}\Big)\ln\frac{K_{\mathrm i}}{K_{\mathrm a}} .
\label{eq:Sn-ar}
\end{equation}
The harmonic law's $\Sigma_t$ is the same calculation with the roles of $K$ and
$1/K$ exchanged (conductivity $\leftrightarrow$ resistivity), which gives
\begin{equation}
\Sigma_t^{\mathrm h}=-\varepsilon\,(K_{\mathrm i}-K_{\mathrm a})\ln\frac{K_{\mathrm i}}{K_{\mathrm a}} .
\label{eq:St-h}
\end{equation}
Both were checked against direct quadrature of \eqref{eq:excess-u}.

\paragraph{Step 4: summary and meaning.}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
law & $\Sigma_t$ (along) & $\Sigma_n$ (across)\\
\midrule
arithmetic & $0$ & $-\varepsilon\,(K_{\mathrm a}^{-1}-K_{\mathrm i}^{-1})\ln(K_{\mathrm i}/K_{\mathrm a})$\\
harmonic & $-\varepsilon\,(K_{\mathrm i}-K_{\mathrm a})\ln(K_{\mathrm i}/K_{\mathrm a})$ & $0$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Both nonzero entries are negative. For the arithmetic law that is a
\emph{missing resistance} across the interface. Put into \eqref{eq:bias} with
its minus sign, it makes the cell conduct too well across interfaces. For the
harmonic law it is a \emph{missing conductance} along the interface, so the cell
conducts too poorly along them. At $K_{\mathrm i}/K_{\mathrm a}=114.5$ the
constants are $234.96\,\varepsilon$~m\,K\,W$^{-1}$ and
$10.76\,\varepsilon$~W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$.

No scalar law can make both entries zero: by Step~2 that would need $K$ and
$1/K$ to be affine in $\varphi$ at the same time, and only a constant is. A
direction-dependent (tensor) coefficient can, since it only needs $K$ affine
along the interface and $1/K$ affine across it. That is the next section.

\section{The tensor law}

Use each law in the direction where its excess vanishes:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;
\bK(\varphi,\nabla\varphi)=\Kar(\varphi)\,\big(\bI-\bn\otimes\bn\big)
+\Kh(\varphi)\,\bn\otimes\bn,
\qquad \bn=\frac{\nabla\varphi}{|\nabla\varphi|}.\;}
\label{eq:tensor}
\end{equation}
Then $K_t=\Kar$ and $K_n=\Kh$ in \eqref{eq:excess}, so
\begin{equation}
\Sigma_t=\Sigma_n=0,
\qquad
\Keff^{\varepsilon}-\Keff^{\star}=O(\varepsilon^2).
\label{eq:second}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Interpretation: a microscopic laminate.} \eqref{eq:tensor} is not an
ad hoc blend. It is the exact effective conductivity of a finely layered
mixture of ice and air, with ice fraction $\varphi$, whose layers lie parallel
to the interface (layer normal $\bn$). For such a laminate, conduction along the
layers is the arithmetic mean and conduction across them is the harmonic mean
(Wiener's bounds; Milton 2002, ch.~9). The tensor law therefore models each
point of the band as a laminate oriented with the interface, which is what the
band locally is.

\paragraph{Properties.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Same sharp problem.} At $\varphi\in\{0,1\}$ both branches equal
$K_\star$, so $\bK=K_\star\bI$ in the bulk and the sharp limit is untouched
(companion note, Lemma~1). Only the band changes.
\item \emph{Well posed.} $\bK$ is symmetric with eigenvalues $\Kar$ (tangential)
and $\Kh$ (normal), both in $[\Ka,\Ki]$. The cell problem remains uniformly
elliptic with the same bounds, and its discrete operator remains symmetric
positive definite.
\item \emph{No free parameters.} Nothing is fitted or calibrated. The law
follows from the two excess conditions alone and holds for any
$\Ki/\Ka$.
\item \emph{Undefined normal is harmless.} $\bn$ is undefined where
$\nabla\varphi=0$, which happens in the bulk, where $\Kar=\Kh$ and $\bK$ is
isotropic whatever $\bn$ is. We set $\bK=\Kar\bI$ there.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Why this is legitimate}

The corrector problem \eqref{eq:cell} is post-processing of a \emph{frozen}
microstructure. The phase-field evolution (vapour transport, latent heat,
interface kinetics) is solved with its own interpolation and is unaffected. The
two interpolations answer different questions. The evolution law sets how the
geometry develops; the homogenization law sets how a given geometry is turned
into $\Keff$. Only the second is changed. The same laws already give the same
sharp problem, so the change amounts to choosing, among the band
representations of that problem, the one with no first-order error.

This parallels the thin-interface corrections of Karma \& Rappel (1998), Almgren
(1999) and Kaempfer \& Plapp (2009), which remove $O(\varepsilon)$ artifacts
from phase-field \emph{dynamics} (for example, with an anti-trapping current or
a renormalized kinetic coefficient). Here the problem is static and has no
source term, so the thin-interface analysis stops at the two equilibrium excesses
\eqref{eq:excess}, and it closes in elementary form. The comparison is one of method only. The
tensor law is a property of the steady-state homogenization, and it is not an
argument for changing the energy balance of any metamorphism model.

\section{Verification}

Two cells with sharp closed forms, run over an $\varepsilon$ ladder at fixed
$\varepsilon/h=5.12$ (so the interface bias is not confounded with mesh
convergence):

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Laminate} ($\bar\varphi=\tfrac12$, $n_\Gam=2$). Along the layers
$k_\parallel=\avg{\Kar}$ is exact for both laws. Across the layers
$1/k_\perp=\avg{1/K_n}$ is linear in $\varepsilon$ with slope
$(n_\Gam/L)\,|\Sigma_n|/\varepsilon$ for the arithmetic law, and exactly flat
at the sharp value for the tensor law. The laminate's normal is a fixed
coordinate axis, so this case does not exercise the off-diagonal part of
\eqref{eq:tensor}.
\item \emph{Disk array} (square array, $f=\pi R^2/L^2=0.196$). The sharp value
is Rayleigh's, $k=0.0295684$~W\,m$^{-1}$K$^{-1}$ (Perrins et al.\ 1979); Maxwell-Garnett
agrees to $1.8\times10^{-4}$. Here the normal takes every direction. Using the
dipole field in \eqref{eq:bias} gives the arithmetic law's slope with no fitted
constants,
\begin{equation}
\frac{dk}{d\varepsilon}=\frac{|\Sigma_n|}{\varepsilon}\,\frac{\pi R}{L^2}
\left[\frac{\Ka(1+\beta)}{1-\beta f}\right]^2,\qquad
\beta=\frac{\Ki-\Ka}{\Ki+\Ka},
\label{eq:diskslope}
\end{equation}
which is $+15\%$ at $\varepsilon=L/100$ and $+1.9\%$ at $L/800$. The disk
ladder starts at $L/100$: at $L/50$, $\varepsilon/R=0.08$ and the visible band
spans most of the radius, outside the thin-interface regime (measured there:
arithmetic $+46\%$, tensor $+6.7\%$).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope and limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Residual $O(\varepsilon^2)$.} This includes curvature terms and a
geometric one: a diffuse disk holds $\pi^3\varepsilon^2/3$ more area of ice
than the sharp disk.
\item \emph{Thin ligaments.} The derivation assumes the band is a flat 1D layer,
which fails where two interfaces are closer than the band width (about
$6$--$9\,\varepsilon$), as in young sinter necks. No interpolation law is
justified there. This limits how well the phase field resolves the geometry,
and it applies to the evolution model's geometry as much as to $\Keff$.
\item \emph{Profile.} The zero excesses use the antisymmetry of the logistic
profile. Any profile with $\varphi(-s)=1-\varphi(s)$ about the
$\varphi=\tfrac12$ level set gives the same result; a profile distorted by the
evolution changes it only at the next order.
\item \emph{Geometry error is untouched.} The law removes the bias of
homogenizing a given $\varphi$. It does not correct errors in $\varphi$ itself.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

The ingredients are established; their combination for this cell problem, with
the closed-form excesses, is what this note adds.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Laminates and mixing bounds.} Arithmetic along and harmonic across
layers is Wiener's classical result. Milton (2002) and Torquato (2002) are the
standard texts on homogenization, laminates and bounds on $\Keff$.
\item \emph{Thin interphases as imperfect interfaces.} A thin layer replaced by
a sharp interface with jump conditions, to first order in its thickness, is
developed for conduction by Hashin (2001), in general form by Benveniste
(2006a), and for curved conducting interphases to arbitrary order in the
thickness by Benveniste (2006b). Eq.~\eqref{eq:bias} is the effective-property consequence of that
replacement.
\item \emph{Excess quantities in thin-interface phase-field analysis.} Karma \&
Rappel (1998), Almgren (1999), McFadden, Wheeler \& Anderson (2000) and Elder
et al.\ (2001) show that $O(\varepsilon)$ errors of diffuse-interface models are
integrals of the form \eqref{eq:excess}, and that interpolation functions can be
chosen to make them vanish. McFadden et al.\ treat unequal conductivities in
particular.
\item \emph{Tensor (rank-one) interpolation in phase-field models.} The closest
analogue is in phase-field elasticity. Durga, Wollants \& Moelans (2013) show
that Voigt and Reuss interpolation of stiffness create interfacial excess
energies. Mosler, Shchyglo \& Montazer Hojjat (2014) and Schneider et al.\
(2015) remove them by interpolating differently along and across $\bn$, so the
mechanical jump conditions hold inside the band. That is continuous normal
traction and tangential strain, the elastic counterparts of continuous $q_n$
and $\mathbf E_t$ here.
\item \emph{Diffuse-domain methods.} Li et al.\ (2009) and Lervåg \& Lowengrub
(2015) analyse elliptic problems posed with a smoothed indicator and show, by
matched asymptotics, which formulations are first- or second-order accurate in
$\varepsilon$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Suggested Methods text}

\begin{quote}\small
The effective conductivity tensor is obtained by periodic homogenization
(Calonne et al., 2011), solving the corrector problem on the frozen phase field
at each sampled time. Inside the diffuse interface the conductivity must be
interpolated. A scalar interpolation carries a first-order bias in the
interface width $\varepsilon$: to first order the band behaves as a sharp
interface with an excess tangential conductance $\Sigma_t$ and an excess normal
resistance $\Sigma_n$ (Hashin, 2001; Benveniste, 2006a). For the logistic
profile, the arithmetic law gives $\Sigma_t=0$ but
$\Sigma_n=-\varepsilon(K_a^{-1}-K_i^{-1})\ln(K_i/K_a)$, which overestimates
conduction across interfaces by up to tens of percent at
$K_i/K_a\approx115$. The harmonic law has the opposite defect. We therefore
use the tensor law
$\mathbf K=K_{\mathrm{ar}}(\varphi)(\mathbf I-\hat{\mathbf n}\otimes\hat{\mathbf n})
+K_{\mathrm h}(\varphi)\,\hat{\mathbf n}\otimes\hat{\mathbf n}$,
$\hat{\mathbf n}=\nabla\varphi/|\nabla\varphi|$, whose excesses both vanish,
so the error is $O(\varepsilon^2)$. It reduces to the sharp conductivities in
each bulk phase, has no adjustable parameters, and affects only the
homogenization step, not the phase-field evolution. We verified it against
closed-form sharp solutions for a laminate and for a square array of disks
(Rayleigh, 1892; Perrins et al., 1979).
\end{quote}
