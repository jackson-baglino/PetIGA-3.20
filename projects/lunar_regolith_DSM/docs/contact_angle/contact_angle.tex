\title{\vspace{-1.4cm}\textbf{Prescribing the ice--regolith contact angle}\\
\large Wall free energy, its weak form, and why no surface energy survives
into the residual coefficient}
\author{\normalsize \code{lunar\_regolith\_DSM}}
\date{\normalsize 2026-09-16}

\begin{document}\maketitle\vspace{-0.7cm}

\begin{center}\fbox{\parbox{0.94\textwidth}{\small
\textbf{Summary.} The substrate in this two-phase model is the domain wall, not a
phase field, so it must act through a boundary condition. Adding Cahn's wall free
energy $f_w(\phi)=\gas+(\gis-\gas)h(\phi)$ to the functional supplies a natural
condition on $\dn\phi$ that fixes the contact angle. Carrying it through the weak
form gives a residual term
$-3M\cos\theta\,h'(\phi)/6$, with no $\eps$ and no surface energy in the
coefficient. Both cancellations are derived below. The $\gia$ that appears in
$\cos\theta$ is \emph{not} the one that cancels.}}\end{center}

\section{What the wall energy is}

Let $\phi$ be the ice fraction, $\phi=1$ ice and $\phi=0$ vapour, and let
$\Gamma$ be the part of $\partial\Omega$ that represents regolith. The wall
carries an energy per unit area that interpolates between its two dry states,
\begin{equation}
f_w(\phi) \;=\; \gas\bigl(1-h(\phi)\bigr) \;+\; \gis\,h(\phi)
\;=\; \gas + (\gis-\gas)\,h(\phi),
\qquad h(\phi)=\phi^{2}(3-2\phi),
\label{eq:fw}
\end{equation}
so $f_w(0)=\gas$ (wall against vapour) and $f_w(1)=\gis$ (wall against ice).

Two properties of $h$ matter. $h(0)=0$ and $h(1)=1$ give the correct end states.
$h'(0)=h'(1)=0$ makes the term inert in both bulk phases: it can only act where
the ice--vapour interface actually meets the wall, and cannot shift the bulk
equilibria of the double well. A linear interpolant would violate the second
property and bias $\phi$ everywhere along $\Gamma$.

\textbf{Note that \eqref{eq:fw} contains no $\gia$.} Only the two
substrate energies appear. This is worth holding onto; $\gia$ enters later, from
a different direction, and that is the whole reason $\cos\theta$ emerges.

\section{The functional the solver actually evolves}

The ice residual implemented in \code{assembly.c} is
\begin{equation}
R \;=\; N\,\phi_t \;+\; 3M\eps\,\nabla N\!\cdot\!\nabla\phi
\;+\; \frac{3M}{\eps}\,f_1(\phi)\,N \;-\; \text{(source)},
\qquad f_1 = W'(\phi),
\label{eq:Rint}
\end{equation}
with $M=\code{mob\_sub}$ and the deliberately half-normalised double well
\begin{equation}
W(\phi)=\tfrac12\,\phi^{2}(1-\phi)^{2}.
\label{eq:W}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:Rint} is the weak form of the gradient flow
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} \;=\; -\,\frac{3M}{\eps}\,
\frac{\delta\Ft}{\delta\phi},
\qquad
\Ft[\phi] \;=\; \int_\Omega\Bigl[\tfrac{\eps^{2}}{2}\,|\nabla\phi|^{2} + W(\phi)\Bigr]\,d\Omega .
\label{eq:Ftilde}
\end{equation}

$\Ft$ is a \emph{shape} functional. It carries no surface energy: there is no
$\gamma$ anywhere in \eqref{eq:Ftilde}, and none in \eqref{eq:Rint}. In this
solver $\gia$ reaches the dynamics only through the kinetic calibration
$d_{0,\mathrm{sub0}}\to\lambda_{\mathrm{sub}}\to\tau_{\mathrm{sub}}\to
M,\alpha_{\mathrm{sub}}$, never as an energy scale multiplying the PDE. A
physical energy therefore cannot be added to \eqref{eq:Ftilde} directly. It has
to be converted first, and the conversion factor is where the interesting
algebra lives.

\subsection{The conversion factor, and where $1/6$ comes from}

For the equilibrium profile of \eqref{eq:W},
\begin{equation}
\phi(x)=\tfrac12\Bigl(1+\tanh\tfrac{x}{2\eps}\Bigr),
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\phi'=\frac{\phi(1-\phi)}{\eps},
\label{eq:profile}
\end{equation}
the first integral of $\eps^{2}\phi''=W'(\phi)$ is $\tfrac{\eps^{2}}{2}\phi'^{2}=W$,
i.e.\ the two terms of the integrand are equal (equipartition). Hence the
interfacial excess of $\Ft$ per unit area is
\begin{equation}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\Bigl[\tfrac{\eps^{2}}{2}\phi'^{2}+W\Bigr]dx
=\eps^{2}\!\int\!\phi'^{2}dx
=\frac{\eps^{2}}{16\eps^{2}}\!\int\!\operatorname{sech}^{4}\!\tfrac{x}{2\eps}\,dx
=\frac{1}{16}\cdot 2\eps\cdot\frac{4}{3}
=\boxed{\frac{\eps}{6}} .
\label{eq:excess}
\end{equation}
(Verified numerically to eight digits.) The same interface carries physical
energy $\gia$ per unit area. The two functionals are therefore related by a pure
number times $\gia/\eps$,
\begin{equation}
\Fp \;=\; \frac{6\gia}{\eps}\,\Ft
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\text{a physical energy $E$ enters $\Ft$ as } \widetilde{E}=\frac{\eps}{6\gia}\,E .
\label{eq:convert}
\end{equation}

\section{The weak form, in full}

Write the total functional including the wall term,
\begin{equation}
\Ft_{\text{tot}}[\phi]
=\int_\Omega\Bigl[\tfrac{\eps^{2}}{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}+W(\phi)\Bigr]d\Omega
\;+\;\oint_\Gamma \widetilde{f}_w(\phi)\,d\Gamma,
\qquad
\widetilde{f}_w=\frac{\eps}{6\gia}f_w .
\end{equation}

Take the first variation with an arbitrary test function $\eta$:
\begin{equation}
\delta\Ft_{\text{tot}}[\eta]
=\int_\Omega\Bigl[\eps^{2}\,\nabla\phi\!\cdot\!\nabla\eta + W'(\phi)\,\eta\Bigr]d\Omega
\;+\;\oint_\Gamma \widetilde{f}_w{}'(\phi)\,\eta\,d\Gamma .
\label{eq:var}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Strong form and the natural boundary condition}

To read off the boundary condition, integrate the gradient term by parts:
\begin{equation}
\int_\Omega \eps^{2}\nabla\phi\!\cdot\!\nabla\eta \,d\Omega
= -\int_\Omega \eps^{2}\nabla^{2}\phi\;\eta \,d\Omega
\;+\;\oint_{\partial\Omega}\eps^{2}\,(\nabla\phi\!\cdot\!\mathbf{n})\,\eta\,dS .
\end{equation}
Substituting into \eqref{eq:var} and collecting,
\begin{equation}
\delta\Ft_{\text{tot}}[\eta]
=\int_\Omega\Bigl[-\eps^{2}\nabla^{2}\phi+W'(\phi)\Bigr]\eta\,d\Omega
\;+\;\oint_{\partial\Omega}\Bigl[\eps^{2}\dn\phi+\widetilde{f}_w{}'(\phi)\Bigr]\eta\,dS .
\end{equation}
Because $\eta$ is arbitrary in $\Omega$ and independently arbitrary on
$\partial\Omega$, stationarity requires both brackets to vanish. The second is
the natural boundary condition,
\begin{equation}
\eps^{2}\,\dn\phi + \widetilde{f}_w{}'(\phi) = 0 .
\label{eq:natural}
\end{equation}
On any face where no wall term is declared, $\widetilde{f}_w{}'\equiv0$ and
\eqref{eq:natural} reduces to $\dn\phi=0$: the zero-flux condition the solver
had everywhere before this feature, which is a $90^\circ$ contact angle imposed
by omission rather than by choice.

\subsection{Evaluating $\widetilde{f}_w{}'$, and the appearance of $\cos\theta$}

From \eqref{eq:fw} and \eqref{eq:convert},
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{f}_w{}'(\phi)
=\frac{\eps}{6\gia}\,(\gis-\gas)\,h'(\phi)
=-\frac{\eps}{6}\underbrace{\frac{\gas-\gis}{\gia}}_{\textstyle \cos\theta}h'(\phi)
=-\frac{\eps}{6}\cos\theta\; h'(\phi).
\label{eq:fwprime}
\end{equation}

This is the step worth pausing on. \textbf{Young's equation was not assumed.} The
wall energy \eqref{eq:fw} contains only $\gas$ and $\gis$; $\gia$ entered solely
through the normalisation \eqref{eq:convert} of the \emph{bulk} functional. Their
ratio is $(\gas-\gis)/\gia$, which \emph{is} Young's $\cos\theta$. The
non-dimensionalisation produces it.

\subsection{The contact angle this enforces}

Insert \eqref{eq:fwprime} into \eqref{eq:natural}:
\begin{equation}
\eps^{2}\dn\phi=\frac{\eps}{6}\cos\theta\,h'(\phi)
=\eps\cos\theta\,\phi(1-\phi)
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\dn\phi=\cos\theta\,\frac{\phi(1-\phi)}{\eps},
\label{eq:bc}
\end{equation}
using $h'(\phi)/6=\phi(1-\phi)$. By \eqref{eq:profile} the right-hand side is
$\cos\theta\,|\nabla\phi|$, and writing $\nabla\phi=|\nabla\phi|\,\mathbf{m}$
with $\mathbf{m}$ the interface normal pointing into the ice,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{n}=\cos\theta\;}
\end{equation}
with $\mathbf{n}$ the outward wall normal: the contact angle measured through the
ice. Limits check: $\cos\theta=+1$ puts $\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{n}$, the interface
normal pointing straight out of the wall, i.e.\ ice sheeted flat onto it,
$\theta=0$; $\cos\theta=-1$ gives $\theta=180^\circ$, a point contact.

\section{The residual and Jacobian terms}

The Galerkin form does \emph{not} integrate the gradient term by parts --- the
integration by parts above was only to expose the boundary condition. The weak
residual is \eqref{eq:var} with $\eta=N_a$, multiplied by the prefactor
$3M/\eps$ of the gradient flow \eqref{eq:Ftilde}:
\begin{equation}
R_a=\int_\Omega N_a\phi_t\,d\Omega
+\frac{3M}{\eps}\Bigl\{\int_\Omega\bigl[\eps^{2}\nabla N_a\!\cdot\!\nabla\phi+W'N_a\bigr]d\Omega
+\oint_\Gamma \widetilde{f}_w{}'(\phi)N_a\,d\Gamma\Bigr\}-\text{(source)} .
\end{equation}
The two volume terms reproduce $3M\eps$ and $3M/\eps$ in \eqref{eq:Rint} exactly.
The boundary term gives, using \eqref{eq:fwprime},
\begin{equation}
R^{\Gamma}_a=\frac{3M}{\eps}\Bigl(-\frac{\eps}{6}\cos\theta\,h'(\phi)\Bigr)N_a
=\boxed{\;-\,3M\,\cos\theta\,\frac{h'(\phi)}{6}\,N_a\;}
\label{eq:Rbnd}
\end{equation}
and, since the wall energy has no $\phi_t$ dependence, a Jacobian with no shift
term,
\begin{equation}
J^{\Gamma}_{ab}=\frac{\partial R^{\Gamma}_a}{\partial\phi_b}
=-\,3M\cos\theta\,\frac{h''(\phi)}{6}\,N_aN_b ,
\qquad h''(\phi)=6(1-2\phi).
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:Rbnd} is \code{assembly.c} verbatim:
\code{Rb[a][0] = -rwb * 3.0 * mob\_b * costhet * (dh/6.0) * N0b[a]}.

\subsection{Why one cannot simply ``keep the $\gia$''}

A natural objection: the hand derivation gives
$\delta f_w/\delta\phi=-\gia\cos\theta\,h'$, so why does $\gia$ not multiply the
residual?

Because that expression already contains $\gia$ twice in disguise. Writing
$f_w=\gas-\gia\cos\theta\,h$ substitutes Young's equation into \eqref{eq:fw}; the
$\gia$ introduced there is cancelled by the $\gia$ in $\cos\theta$, leaving
$(\gis-\gas)h$, which is \eqref{eq:fw} again. The \emph{only} $\gia$ with
independent standing is the one in the normalisation \eqref{eq:convert}, and it
is that one which converts $(\gas-\gis)$ into $\cos\theta$.

There is a real physical assumption buried here, and it is worth naming: the
$\gia$ in \eqref{eq:convert} is the ice--vapour energy the \emph{bulk} model
realises, while the $\gia$ one might write in $f_w$ is the ice--vapour energy at
the \emph{contact line}. They are the same interface, so they are the same
number, and the ratio is one. If they were allowed to differ --- as a
sensitivity test, say --- the ratio would survive and $\gia$ would reappear
explicitly.

\subsection{Independent check}

\code{demo/Metamorph.c} in the same PetIGA tree carries a contact-angle term
derived by a different route,
\code{R[a][0] = -N0[a]*3.0*eps*mob*|grad phi|*costhet}. Since
$\eps|\nabla\phi|=\phi(1-\phi)=h'(\phi)/6$ on the equilibrium profile
\eqref{eq:profile}, that is \eqref{eq:Rbnd} exactly. Two derivations, one
coefficient.

The $h(\phi)$ form is nonetheless preferable: it needs no $1/|\nabla\phi|$
regularisation (Metamorph clamps $|\nabla\phi|$ at $10^{-5}$ to avoid dividing by
zero in its Jacobian), its Jacobian is an exact mass-matrix block, and it remains
correct away from the equilibrium profile, where
$\eps|\nabla\phi|\neq\phi(1-\phi)$.

\section{Clamping, and why it is required}

$h'(\phi)=6\phi(1-\phi)$ changes sign for $\phi<0$ and $\phi>1$. So does
\eqref{eq:Rbnd}, and since $R=N\phi_t+\dots=0$, a sign-flipped wall term drives
$\phi$ \emph{further} out of range: a runaway.

Nothing in the bulk opposes it. The interior form evaluates $f_1$ and the
localiser at the clamped $\phi_c\in[0,1]$, so for $\phi<0$ both are identically
zero --- there is no restoring force outside the range by construction. An
unclamped wall term is then the only term acting there, and it acts outward.

This is not hypothetical. At $\eps/R=1/100$ a $-2\times10^{-6}$ excursion at the
wall grew to $-0.05$ within a few hundred steps, crossed \code{-phase\_lo}, and
the residual's domain guard then returned a zero residual, which \code{SNES} read
as $\|F\|=0$ and reported as converged. The solution froze for the remaining
23{,}370 steps while the clock ran on to $t_{\text{final}}$ and the run reported
success. The implementation therefore clamps $\phi$ to $[0,1]$ before evaluating
$h'$, with the Jacobian clamping identically and carrying the chain-rule factor
$d\phi_c/d\phi$, so that it remains an exact Jacobian of the clamped residual.

\section{Using it}

\begin{center}\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
\code{-wall\_faces "y0,y1"} & which domain faces are regolith \\
\code{-gamma\_is} & ice--regolith surface energy [J/m$^2$] \\
\code{-gamma\_as} & air--regolith surface energy [J/m$^2$] \\
\code{-gamma\_ia} & ice--air; default $(\Sigma_i+\Sigma_a)/2$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{center}

The solver forms $\cos\theta=(\gas-\gis)/\gia$, refuses the run if
$|\gas-\gis|>\gia$ (no angle exists; one phase wets completely), and prints both
the energies and the derived $\theta$.

\textbf{On the default.} $\gia$ defaults to $(\Sigma_i+\Sigma_a)/2$, not to
$\Sigma_i$. In the convention these projects inherit,
$\Sigma_i=\gia+\gis-\gas$ and $\Sigma_a=\gia+\gas-\gis$, so
$\Sigma_i+\Sigma_a=2\gia$ and the ice--air energy is their mean --- which is also
what the solver's own double-well coefficient $C=(\Sigma_i+\Sigma_a)/2+\Lambda$
says. $\Sigma_i$ alone equals $\gia$ only when $\Sigma_a=\Sigma_i$, which
\code{enceladus\_DSM} enforces and \code{lunar\_regolith\_DSM} does not; with the
lunar defaults $(0.109,0.132)$ taking $\Sigma_i$ understates $\gia$ by $10.6\%$,
and that propagates directly into $\theta$. Pass \code{-gamma\_ia} explicitly
whenever the substrate matters.

The only admissibility constraint is $|\gas-\gis|\le\gia$. The three-phase
triple-well constraint $\gis>\tfrac{\gia}{2}(1-\cos\theta)$ does \emph{not}
apply: it came from requiring a positive $\Sigma_s$, and there is no sediment
phase and no triple well here.

\section{Validation}

Five prescribed angles from $30^\circ$ to $150^\circ$ in a flat channel recover
Young's prediction with a maximum error of $0.127^\circ$ and an RMS of
$0.062^\circ$, against a $2^\circ$ acceptance criterion, with the error falling
as $\eps/R$ is refined. A droplet on a single wall, which has no confinement,
agrees with the channel, so the angle is set by the boundary condition rather
than by the geometry. Details in
\code{studies/contact\_angle/}.
