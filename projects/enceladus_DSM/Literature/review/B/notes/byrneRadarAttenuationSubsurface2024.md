# byrneRadarAttenuationSubsurface2024 — Byrne, Plesa, Rückriemen-Bez, Hussmann & Benedikter (2024), JGR Planets 129, e2024JE008615, doi:10.1029/2024JE008615 (printed p.1)

**Summary.** 1-D conductive ice-shell temperature profiles (k(T) = 651/T) beneath a porous surface layer of constant conductivity (0.001–0.1 W/m/K, thickness 0–700 m), with radar attenuation from Matsuoka/Corr (low-loss: pure ice; high-loss: 300 µM Cl). Shells are 5–35 km thick, surface 60 K (85 K tested). Global distributions of layer thickness are also explored.

**Findings relevant to the manuscript**
- p.2: "we consider the effects of a porous ice layer specifically as a thermal insulator ... Thermal conductivity values have been measured for porous icy layers in laboratory experiments (Seiferlin et al., 1996) and derived from thermophysical modeling (Ferrari et al., 2021), yielding values between 0.025 and 0.001 Wm−1K−1."
- p.3: "we vary the thermal conductivity of this porous surface layer between 0.001 and 0.1 Wm−1K−1. Note, that thermal conductivity is assumed to be constant in the porous layer".
- p.1 abstract: "as little as 2% of the ice shell can be penetrated", but "a thick porous surface layer leads to high subsurface temperatures promoting the formation of brines at shallow depth that can be detected".
- p.9: "Another important factor that will affect the thermal state of the ice shell and hence the two-way attenuation is the thermal conductivity of the porous surface layer." Conclusion: the layer "has a first order effect on the two-way radar attenuation".
- p.8: south polar surface "about 85 K on average ... with values higher than 145 K in localized areas close to the tiger stripes."
- Sintering is not discussed (text search).
