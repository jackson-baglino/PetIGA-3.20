# spencerEnceladusActiveIce2013 — Spencer & Nimmo (2013), Annu. Rev. Earth Planet. Sci. 41, 693–717

**Summary.** Review of Cassini results on Enceladus: geology, south polar terrain (SPT) and tiger stripes, thermal emission, plume structure/composition, surface, interior, tidal heating, habitability.

**Findings relevant to the manuscript**
- Tiger-stripe temperature, PDF p.8 (journal 700): "surface temperatures within the troughs reach at least 180 K over regions tens of meters wide along the length of the tiger stripes (Spencer et al. 2009, 2011)". Also p.18 (710): thermal signature "consistent with conductive heating of the surface by fractures with temperatures above 200 K (Abramov & Spencer 2009)".
- Tiger stripes: ~130 km long, spaced 35 km, central trough ~2 km wide, 0.5 km deep (p.7–8).
- Plume, p.8–9 (700–701): "Multiple jets of vapor and particles erupt from the tiger stripes ... These then combine at altitude to form a merged plume" (citing Spitale & Porco 2007). "The plume particles are predominantly water ice, but most contain approximately 1% salt, mostly NaCl (Postberg et al. 2011), and have typical radii of a few micrometers." Only ~10 % of the dust escapes; most "fall back" (p.9).
- Surface, p.9–10 (701–702): high albedo "due to a surface coating of reimpacted ice-rich plume particles (Kempf et al. 2010)"; "The largest [ice-grain] sizes are found near the tiger stripes (Jaumann et al. 2008)"; "Surface temperatures at low latitudes vary from ∼50 K at night to ∼80 K during the day (Howett et al. 2010). The diurnal temperature variation implies a very low surface thermal conductivity and a fluffy unconsolidated surface, as expected for plume particle fallout".

**Claims supported / qualified**
- Draft L259 "Temperatures near Tiger Stripes may reach up to 180 K (Spencer & Nimmo 2013)" and outline L335–336: **partially** — the source says "at least 180 K" within the troughs over regions tens of metres wide (and fractures >200 K). "up to" inverts the bound. Fix: "surface temperatures within the tiger-stripe troughs reach at least 180 K (Spencer & Nimmo, 2013)".
- Outline §5.5 (L1329–1331): "Tiger Stripe temperatures up to about 180 K (Spencer); grain size increasing toward the fractures (Jaumann)" — same correction on temperature; grain size claim supported here (p.10) as a review statement citing Jaumann.
- Outline 3a (plume grains micron-sized, mostly water ice, percent-level NaCl): supported also by p.9 ("approximately 1% salt, mostly NaCl", "radii of a few micrometers").
- Outline 3b "deposits remain poorly consolidated" — consistent with p.10 "fluffy unconsolidated surface" inferred from thermal inertia (an observation, independent of Choukroun's lab extrapolation).

**Candidate additions.** (i) In Intro ¶3b/§5.5, Spencer & Nimmo p.10 is a direct observational support that the low-latitude surface is a low-conductivity, unconsolidated layer of plume fallout (diurnal temperatures 50–80 K) — closely matches "A porous surface layer insulates the ice shell". (ii) Correct "up to 180 K" to "at least 180 K".
