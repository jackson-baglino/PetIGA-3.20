# schweizerSnowAvalancheFormation2003 — Schweizer, Jamieson & Schneebeli, Rev. Geophys. 41(4), 1016 (2003)
DOI (PDF p.1): 10.1029/2002RG000123. Read in full (25 pp; reference list skimmed).

## Summary
Review of dry-snow slab avalanche formation: contributory factors (terrain, new snow, wind, temperature, stratigraphy), slab-release and fracture-mechanics models, microstructure, snow failure (damage vs sintering), fracture propagation, spatial variability.

## Findings relevant to the manuscript
- Persistent weak layers: "Climax avalanches follow from old snow instabilities due to the failure of a buried layer of kinetic growth crystals. These crystal types include surface hoar, (near-surface) faceted crystals, and depth hoar, and they compose persistent weak layers" (p.1–2, printed 2-1/2-2).
- "Snow cover stratigraphy is recognized as the key contributing factor for dry snow slab avalanche formation ... the weak layer or interface is a necessary prerequisite but not sufficient condition" (p.8–9/2-8–2-9).
- "70% of 186 skier-triggered avalanches were released by weak layers of persistent grain types (i.e., surface hoar, faceted crystals, and depth hoar)" (p.9/2-9).
- Near-surface faceting from large temperature gradients (150 K m⁻¹, growth ~0.1 mm d⁻¹) (p.9/2-9).
- Snow "is a sintered material" (p.14/2-14); failure governed by competition of bond fracture and sintering, sintering "highly temperature-dependent" (p.15/2-15).
- Weak layers "usually have fewer bonds per grain ... form layers of low strength" (p.14/2-14).

## Claims supported / contradicted / qualified
- Draft L256 "Depth hoar formation under strong temperature gradients creates a mechanically weak layer within snowpacks and drives slab avalanche initiation": supported as "contributes to" — depth hoar is one of the persistent weak-layer types that release slab avalanches (p.2/2-2; p.9/2-9). "drives" overstates: a weak layer is "a necessary prerequisite but not sufficient" (p.8/2-8). Note also surface hoar is the more emphasised weak layer in this review.
- Draft L253 "unshoveled snow seems to set like concrete" (no citation): this review supports that snow is a sintered material whose strength grows by bond formation (p.14–15).
