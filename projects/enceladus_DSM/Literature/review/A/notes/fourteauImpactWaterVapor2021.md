# fourteauImpactWaterVapor2021 — Fourteau, Domine & Hagenmuller 2021, The Cryosphere 15:2739–2755 (doi 10.5194/tc-15-2739-2021)

**Summary.** Theory + FE simulations (Elmer, 34 μCT snow samples, 223–273 K, imposed 50 K m^-1) of how vapor diffusion and latent heat enter the effective thermal conductivity. Two limits: slow kinetics (small α: snow is inert, K_eff = conduction only, as in Calonne 2011) and fast kinetics (α → ∞: pore air acts with apparent conductivity k_v = k_a + βLD0, and latent heat also raises the conductive part). Under a gradient, Curvature effects neglected (TG regime).

**Findings relevant to the manuscript**
- Motivation p.1: thermal conductivity "governs the magnitude of the thermal energy flux through the snowpack ... and it thus plays an integral role in the energy budgets of the ground (Zhang et al., 1996), ice caps and glaciers (Gilbert et al., 2012), and sea ice ..., as well as in the temperature of the snow surface"; "variations of thermal conductivity between snow layers impact the temperature gradients at the layer scale and thus in part govern snow metamorphism (Vionnet et al., 2012)."
- Slow kinetics = conduction only p.3 (2741): "snow can be viewed as an inert medium for heat conduction. This slow kinetics case has been treated in detail by Calonne et al. (2011)".
- Size of the vapor contribution p.8 (2746): "Near the melting point (Fig. 6b), the fast kinetics effective thermal conductivity is between 10 % and 50 % higher than in the slow kinetics case. For colder snow at 248 K (Fig. 6a), the relative difference is less marked and ranges from 1 % to 10 %."; larger for low-density snow; abstract "up to about 50 % for low-density snow".
- Which limit applies p.11–12 (2749–2750): "Krol and Löwe (2016) suggest that isothermal metamorphism is slightly better represented by a slow kinetics, while temperature gradient metamorphism data appear consistent with fast kinetics"; for vapor diffusion "the transition between the slow and fast kinetics regimes occurs for sticking coefficients α around 10^−3"; K&P's α range 10^-3–10^-1 "thus within the fast kinetics regime".
- Coupling caveat p.12: "One should therefore be careful when treating heat conduction as decoupled from vapor transport (e.g., Calonne et al., 2011 ...)".
- The k_eff is defined as response to a macroscopic gradient (p.2); steady state at the pore scale justified by 10^6 separation of time scales (p.3).

**Claims it supports / contradicts / qualifies**
- Outline L565–568 ("Fourteau et al. (2021) show that vapor transport under a gradient adds to the apparent conductivity; the conductivity here is conductive only, which is exact for isothermal sintering"): first half **supported** (p.8, abstract). Second half **qualify**: during isothermal sintering there is no macroscopic heat flux, so k_eff is a property for a *subsequently applied* gradient; Fourteau's point is that whether vapor adds to it depends on the kinetics (α), not on how the microstructure was produced. Our cell problem gives the slow-kinetics (conduction-only) value, i.e. the lower bound of Fourteau's Eq. 15; with fast kinetics it could be 1–10% higher at 248 K and 10–50% near 273 K for low-density snow. Since α_c = 10^-3 sits at Fourteau's slow/fast transition, say "the conductive part, which is the full value in the slow-kinetics limit (Fourteau et al., 2021)". Suggested wording in the outline new file.

The figure to quote: up to ~50% (low density, near melting), 1–10% at 248 K.
- Outline L294–297 (Intro P2 proposed sentence: conductivity "sets how fast heat crosses them and so the temperature profile"): Fourteau p.1 supports directly and adds "energy budgets of the ground, ice caps and glaciers ... and the temperature of the snow surface" — a closer citation than Calonne 2011 for that sentence.
